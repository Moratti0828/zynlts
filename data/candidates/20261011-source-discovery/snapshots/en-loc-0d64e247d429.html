My Job: Serena Ying-Ting Chiu and the American Folklife Center

October 7, 2026

Posted by: Mark Hartsell

Share this post:

-“My Job” is a recurring feature that runs across all Library publications. You can also find this story in the September/October issue of the Library of Congress Magazine.

Describe your work at the Library.

As a library technician at the American Folklife Center, I process archival folklife collections in a wide range of formats — manuscripts, photographs, audio recordings, moving images and digital files — and help make them accessible to researchers and the public. I particularly enjoy the multiformat nature of the work, where I can listen to everything from wax cylinder recordings to reel-to-reel tapes to contemporary digital formats.

How did you prepare for your position?

My path to AFC was not a direct one. Originally from Taiwan, I came to the United States to study music at the University of Maryland — I’m a classically trained pianist. When I decided to change careers, I started thinking about working in a library. I felt the two fields share something in common: You can discover hidden treasures in both worlds.

Before joining the Library of Congress, I worked at the National Library of Medicine, where I gained experience working with large-scale collections. At the same time, I still wanted to stay connected to my musical background. An opportunity at AFC immediately felt like the right place for me, where my interests in living traditions, folk culture and performance could come together. In many ways, it connects my path in music with my interest in archives and cultural memory.

What are some of your standout projects?

A highlight has been processing the AIDS Memorial Quilt Records after they were donated by the NAMES Project to AFC in 2019. I helped process more than 150,000 letters, photos, personal mementos and ephemera connected to nearly 50,000 quilt panels memorializing over 110,000 people who lost their lives to HIV/AIDS. I also assisted archives staff members with a digitization project to have these materials presented on the Library’s website. We began work on the collection during the COVID-19 pandemic and had to set up social-distancing protocols to be able to collaborate on processing this collection, which documents another global pandemic.

While processing the collection, I read many moving letters written by family members and loved ones. I was touched by the deep love, grief and resilience of the people and communities affected by the AIDS epidemic. The work gave me a meaningful opportunity to help preserve a collective expression of remembrance through community folk art.

What are your favorite experiences at the Library?

One thing that continues to amaze me about AFC is its kaleidoscope-like environment. AFC brings together so many different voices and traditions — expressive culture, oral traditions, folk and traditional music, foodways, occupational traditions and the diverse cultural practices of American and diaspora communities.

The best part is that I get to experience folklife, not just archive it. Through AFC’s public programs, I have attended a Homegrown concert by an Argentine ethnomusicologist, listened to a rising folk singer-songwriter from Tennessee, attended a Botkin Lecture by the director of the Irish Traditional Music Archive in Dublin and learned about traditions such as Louisiana French music and Thai American culture through the Community Collections Grants Symposium.

Working at AFC feels like being inside a globe and seeing the world right in front of me.
