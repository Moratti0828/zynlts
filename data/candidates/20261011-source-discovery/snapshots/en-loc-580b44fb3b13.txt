Let’s check in with 1982.

“E.T.” landed in theaters and called home. Something called “emoticons” were posted by a computer scientist and so began the creation story of emojis. CDs appeared in record stores. And the Library’s Music Division hired Raymond White, a fresh-faced young man from University of Maryland with degrees in history and vocal performance, to a permanent position.

And lo, for all the days in the world since then until the one at the very end of this month, Ray White has been a fixture at the Library of Congress. He retires at the close of business on Friday, July 31, a mere 44.5 years after he officially started. We are sorry he is leaving so soon but will wish him the best anyhow.

Ray has delighted patrons, scholars and guests of the Library with his knowledge of the field, encompassing everything from Beethoven to Broadway show tunes, including delightful asides and bon mots about great pieces of art or how simple little songs became big hits. Though he does much work behind the scenes, he is best known for his storytelling in presenting items for guests, be they students or VIPs, to marvel over.

We caught up with Ray for a final conversation recently. The following has been lightly edited for space and clarity.

What was your very first assignment at the Library?

Well, before I was hired permanently, I first started as a temporary employee in the summer of 1980. I was to sort a big pile of copyright deposit sheet music in deck 49 of the Jefferson Building in preparation for the Music Division’s move to the Madison Building, which would not occur until December of 1982. It was all quite ho-hum.

Do you still get goosebumps when special stuff comes in?

Oh, absolutely. And I still get goosebumps when I handle treasures that have been here for decades. Representatives of the Gershwin Legacy Project, founded by a cousin of George and Ira, recently visited the Library, as they’re contemplating some commemorative activities for the “An American in Paris” centenary in 2028. “Rhapsody in Blue” had its centenary two years ago. These are both works for which we have all the stuff . For “An American in Paris” we have 22 pages of George Gershwin’s autograph sketches. We have his two-piano score for the entire work. We have his short score for the whole thing and his ink holograph full score, which is right now on display in the Gershwin Gallery.

Is that Gershwin reverence ? Something else?

Part of it’s Gershwin reverence, but part of it is the recognition that we’ve got the entire documentary evidence of the creation of these works. One of the things I tell people about the Library is that we love process. We certainly want to know what the final product was, but we’re as eager to know how it got there. That’s true across the library — the Walt Whitman materials in Manuscript; you go to the Treasures Gallery and see the rough draft of the Declaration of Independence. It’s all the same principle. The rough draft is an excellent example. If the line weren’t so famous — “We hold these truths to be self-evident” — it would be far less impactful to know that Jefferson first wrote, “We hold these truths to be sacred and undeniable.”

That moment of creation … when you see the light switch on, it really humanizes it.

Yes. I also think that people who don’t handle creative drafts on a regular basis might kind of assume that these masterpieces fell down out of the sky from St. Cecilia [the patron saint of music] herself and this is how they are. But no, these are people who got up in the morning and they put on their clothes and they drank their coffee and they did what they did.

Other goosebump favorites?

I would say Bach , Beethoven and Mozart. We have two very, very fine Bach manuscripts — two of his church cantatas. Our best Beethoven is his Piano Sonata Opus 109 . And probably our best Mozart is his Violin Concerto No. 5 in A major , which, by the way, he wrote when he was 19. I’ve shown the Beethoven on several occasions to pianists who have played it and the Bach to choral directors and the Mozart to violinists. They elicit the reaction of “Oh my goodness!”

There’s a particular way you have of showing these things to people.

Well, I mean other colleagues know to do the same thing. When you’re planning for top-drawer performers coming in, you look online, you see what they’ve performed recently, you see what they’ve recorded, what might float their boat and then you see what we have. Denyce Graves Montgomery (the D.C.-born opera star) was here for a Scott Joplin event earlier this year about the Washington National Opera’s new production of Joplin’s “ Treemonisha .” We have a very rare copy of the original printed piano/vocal score of that … and we also have a printed piano/vocal score from the first production of “ Carmen .”

Which was her breakout role.

Yes. And it’s annotated throughout by the first violinist from the initial 20 performances or so with the metronome markings for “How fast does this actually go?” So, we had it on a table and her eyes got as big as saucers and she couldn’t believe it. She was just blown away and posed for pictures next to it.

Do you collect any of these types of things at home? I mean, you have the expertise from the job.

I really don’t. I have some very nice watercolors. My dining room, I have three large watercolors in Venice and they’re by artists whose names appear in the literature. But no, I really don’t collect personally.

Immediate plans for retirement? Usually, people say they’re going to travel.

My immediate plans don’t sound very exciting. I want to go home and clean my house. I want to start getting rid of things I don’t need.

Well then!

The term “travel” is kind of interesting because a lot of people speak of travel with a capital “T,” like a Nile cruise or something. My immediate travel plans are travel with a small “t,” to visit people within driving distance. I have a lot of friends. I’m very fortunate in that regard. So, I plan in the near term to do things I haven’t had time for, which might be a “have lunch with a friend” kind of thing.
