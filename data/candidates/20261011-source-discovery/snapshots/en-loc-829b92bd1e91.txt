—This is a guest post by Judith Lee, the Literacy Awards program manager.

The year was 1980, and in Brazil’s teeming metropolis of Rio de Janeiro, Yvonne Bezerra de Mello started Escola Sem Portas Nem Janelas (Schools Without Doors or Windows). The idea was to offer classes on the sidewalk for kids living on the streets.

Things moved along for more than a decade. Then, in 1993, de Mello was devastated when eight of her students were among those killed by militiamen. The incident, known as the Candelária massacre, was a mass shooting by several gunmen directed at homeless teens and children living on the streets.

She dug in, though, and over the ensuing years de Mello developed her sidewalk school into Projeto Uerê, a multi-faceted program that restores learning capacity to children whose cognitive and emotional development have been disrupted by trauma.

This month, the school won the Library of Congress’ top honor in the 2026 Literacy Awards , the $150,000 David M. Rubenstein Prize , bringing the organization resources and international recognition.

“This honor represents far more than an award,” said de Mello in an interview with the Library. “It is an acknowledgment of the potential of every child to learn, grow and transform his or her future, regardless of poverty, violence or social exclusion.”

The school has worked with some 7,000 children over the past three decades, as its website explains, serving students aged 5-13 who are years behind grade level with severe learning blocks caused by trauma. The school focuses on developing each student’s attention, memory and language processing as a means of restoring basic cognitive functions.

Acting Librarian of Congress Robert Newlen recently announced the award on International Literacy Day, also naming the annual program’s other top three prize winners and 17 honorees . The groups were awarded a total of $495,000, all going to literacy-building nonprofits working around the world.

“The 2026 Literacy Awards winners and honorees have successfully demonstrated thoughtful responses to their learners’ unique circumstances in their approach to literacy education,” Newlen said.

For agencies that often work in difficult circumstances, the awards and recognition hit home.

In Grand Rapids, Michigan, Literacy Center of West Michigan received the $100,000 Kislak Family Foundation prize for their outsized impact on literacy development relative to their small size.

In New York City, Read Alliance received the $50,000 American Prize for helping K-2 students read at grade level while simultaneously providing teen tutors with meaningful paid employment, professional development and leadership training.

Finally, South Africa’s The Nal’ibali Trust received the $50,000 International Prize for distributing over 90 million reading supplements and over 116 bilingual stories to help develop a nation of young readers. The Literacy Awards had recognized the program before, in 2019, as a Best Practices Honoree.

The Library’s Literacy Awards, established in 2013 with Rubenstein’s support (and bolstered by the Kislak Family Foundation in 2023) has awarded 298 prizes, totaling more than $4.8 million. More than 240 organizations from 42 countries have been recognized for their work.
