-This is a guest post by Nyla Adams, an intern in the Office of Communications this summer.

In 1882, a 17-year-old Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge embarked on a grand tour of Europe with her parents, traveling to nearly every country of Western Europe and attending operas whenever possible.

Coolidge kept several journals, including narrative writings and a “botanical diary” of pressed flowers and other greenery representing the places she visited.

Many years after that trip, in 1925, the pianist and composer established the Coolidge Foundation at the Library, providing support for contemporary music to continue through generations, inspiring and shaping the mission of the Library’s Music Division.

Her spectacular album of botanical specimens is a hidden gem preserved in the division’s Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge Foundation Collection, which was fully processed and made available for research last year .

“Alongside her narrative diary and photographs from her European tour, researchers have the opportunity to learn as much about Elizabeth the human as we do about Mrs. Coolidge the benefactor,” said Cait Miller, senior music reference specialist.

The specimens, dating from the late 19th to early 20th century, are accompanied by handwritten notes and labels on the pages describing where they were collected, giving researchers an idea of the notable cities and sites Coolidge experienced.

There are moss and leaves from the graves of poets William Wordsworth and Robert Southey, picked in July. A page full of pressed alpine flowers from Switzerland includes these accompanying notes: “Rhone Glacier (8000 ft),” “Walked fifteen miles” and “Birthday flowers from Mrs. Oakley. Sept. 3.”

Most of the items Coolidge collected are from Europe, where she was traveling, studying and performing — creating an unexpected way to understand Coolidge’s journey.

Starting in August 2025 and finishing earlier this year, conservation technician Anna Zastrow worked diligently to treat, stabilize and house this unique item after consultation with Liz Peirce, an object conservator, and Jen Lewis, a preservation specialist.

Zastrow’s conservation efforts make it possible for staff and researchers to safely engage with the Coolidge materials and ensure they remain accessible into the future.

“The nice thing about these being plant-based is that paper is also plant-based, so there’s an understanding that they are chemically compatible,” Zastrow said about the process of working with botanical items.

At more than 125 years old, the botanical specimens in Coolidge’s album varied in condition prior to treatment. The pieces were affixed to brittle pages that were mostly detached from a poor-quality album, which can put these items at risk for future damage. Each page was removed from the broken volume and mended as needed. Due to the age of the item, some specimens were loose or broken, and these were consolidated and reattached if necessary.

“Botanicals create their own unique challenges in conservation because they are so fragile. We were lucky that the botanicals are in incredible shape,” Zastrow said.

She attached tiny strips of paper, adhered at the edges with a adhesive, as bridges across loose areas of the botanicals to hold them unobtrusively to the pages and maintain stability. Using paper, rather than adhesive, to gently hold the botanicals in place is a safer alternative.

With the treatment complete, the album has been returned to the Music Division.

This 33-page volume is now accessible as a set of that protect the pages set below the mats, allowing them to be viewed and handled without causing further damage — and letting researchers understand the journey of a teenage Coolidge.

“This unique diary allows a youthful Elizabeth’s personality to shine through,” Miller said. “Not only can you chart her travels, but you can glean her own interest by the more specifics sites she chose to document.”

Along with the biographical materials in the Coolidge Foundation Collection, these botanical keepsakes speak to Coolidge’s formative years, giving a new perspective and helping researchers see how a journey through Europe helped inspire this influential figure in music history.
