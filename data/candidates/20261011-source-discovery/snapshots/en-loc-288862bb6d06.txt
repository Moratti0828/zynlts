This is a guest post by Nyla Adams, a recent intern in the Office of Communications.

The Declaration of Independence didn’t just make American pop culture possible — often, it’s been the star of the show.

In “National Treasure,” the 2004 popcorn thriller, Nicolas Cage must somehow steal the Declaration from the National Archives (it has a secret map on the back) to keep it from getting … really stolen. It’s complicated.

Or in “Hamilton,” Lin-Manuel Miranda’s Pulitzer Prize-winning 2015 musical (still on Broadway!), Angelica Schuyler and Thomas Jefferson each rap famous lines from Jefferson’s work on the Declaration.

In the Bicentennial mania of 1976, comic-book readers watched both Superman and Captain American travel back in time to save the Declaration’s origin story.

And on television and cable, the Declaration has popped up (mostly being quoted) in everything from “Star Trek” to “The Andy Griffith Show” to the HBO miniseries “John Adams” to … you get the idea.

The Library’s new exhibit, “ The Declaration’s Promise: A Revolutionary Idea,” takes a sweeping view of the Declaration’s impact, from the finer points of political philosophy to basic human rights. But it also showcases how the document — and its turbulent creation — gave birth to 250 years of pop culture, from the profound to the pedestrian.

It laid a unique foundation for a national culture in which freedom of artistic expression , from the poetry of Walt Whitman and Joy Harjo to the jazz of Louis Armstrong , became a defining American characteristic.

Among the foundational works in this new national expression were Noah Webster’s “The American Spelling Book” in 1783, “ An American Selection of Lessons in Reading and Speaking … ” in 1787 and another spelling book in 1790. (The second book is featured in the exhibit.)

The books, coming in the aftermath of the Declaration’s signing, helped standardize American English, as opposed to its British antecedent. Webster, a Connecticut state representative and author, fiercely believed in the power of written word to help shape the new republic. His books taught generations of young people American pronunciations, moral lessons and patriotic ideals, becoming a widely taught blueprint for American identity.

Which, if we hit the fast forward button, brings us to Kermit the Frog and our foundational star, the Declaration of Independence itself.

In 1982, as the exhibit shows, the Muppets were featured on a major ABC television special, “I Love Liberty.” Each Muppet played a member of the raucous Second Continental Congress, with Kermit playing Thomas Jefferson. The skit demonstrated the many influences at work while creating the Declaration. The Muppet’s creator, Jim Henson, submitted this example of Kermit for copyright protection, and the Muppets have since interpreted the Declaration for audiences on numerous occasions.

Right next to Kermit in the exhibit is the bright, colorful Action Comics #463 from 1976, in which Superman (and his alter ego, Clark Kent) go back in time to save the Declaration. Above that is Marvel Comics’ competing issue, “Captain America’s Bicentennial Battles,” in which Cap also travels back in time to get involved with the war in a personal way — Benjamin Franklin uses his uniform as the basis for the American flag.

From “Hamilton,” there’s the printed piano score of “One Last Time,” a gift to the Library from Miranda, who used the Library’s materials in researching the production.

So, two centuries before celebrity influencers and social media, the commitment to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness helped to establish a nation while simultaneously shaping its culture.
