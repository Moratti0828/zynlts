Ann Patchett accepted the Library’s 2026 Prize for American Fiction at the National Book Festival , on stage with friend and fellow author Tayari Jones and in front of a standing-room-only crowd of more than 1,100 adoring fans, but she wasn’t entirely sure she wanted to be there.

“I feel like I’m too young for lifetime achievement,” she said, drawing a huge laugh from the crowd.

Patchett (62, for the record) overcame her tongue-in-cheek concerns that the honor was “the nail in the coffin” and accepted the award from Robert R. Newlen, the acting Librarian of Congress.

She might have been mollified by his description of one of the Library’s highest honors, given annually since 2008.

“The prize honors an American literary writer whose body of work is distinguished not only for its mastery of the art, but also for the originality and thought and imagination,” Newlen said.

Previous recipients include E.L. Doctorow , Toni Morrison, Philip Roth, Louise Erdrich , George Saunders and last year’s honoree (and Patchett’s close friend), Geraldine Brooks.

Patchett, born in Los Angeles and now a Nashville independent bookstore owner, certainly belongs in that crowd. She’s written 10 novels, five works of nonfiction and three children’s books. Her novels, particularly “Bel Canto” and “The Dutch House,” have won major awards. Her work has been translated into more than 30 languages; she’s been awarded the National Humanities Medal and the PEN/Faulkner Award; and, in 2012, Time Magazine named her to its annual “100 Most Influential People in the World” list.

All that is impressive, but as the American Bookseller’s Association once recognized with its “Most Engaging Author” award, she’s a delightful, generous personality on stage, gently self-deprecating while projecting a sense of candor and warmth.

She and Jones (“An American Marriage”) are longtime friends and exchanged witticisms and stories on stage about the writing process and living their lives around it. National Public Radio’s Mary Louise Kelly moderated the 45-minute conversation.

The central aspect of the conversation was that both authors have new books out, but both struggled with ideas that died beforehand.

“The novel wasn’t noveling,” Jones said of her original idea, though she spent years on it. She eventually went back to basics: “I wrote as a child in a spiral bound notebook with a pencil, not even a pen, a pencil.”

The book that emerged, “Kin,” wasn’t at all what she had first envisioned. But once she got on track, the work flowed.

Patchett spent more than a year developing a sequel of sorts to her previous novel, “Tom Lake,” and grew so frustrated that she passed up a trip to Paris with her husband to stay at home and wrestle with it.

That didn’t work, either. She explained the slow demise of that book, and her loss of faith in it, to her audience in terms of a failed romantic relationship. Here’s the full quote — without a single word or “um or uh” edited out — so you can see why she’s so engaging on stage:

“It’s like a relationship that isn’t working out — you know . And I was writing this novel, but I was also putting the hand mixer in the refrigerator when I had finished using it and thinking, ‘Well, I’m in my 60s now. Maybe this is just that time of life when I start putting the hand mixer in the refrigerator.’

“I was living in the fog. If any of you have ever been in a relationship that is bad and you know that it’s bad — but you can’t even let that knowledge come all the way up to the surface because it’s so painful and it points out all of the time and life you’ve wasted in this relationship that is actually not going to make it. So, you have to clamp your blinders on and move into the fog so that you can bear to live your life. I hope I’m not touching on any nerves here (laughter from audience), but clearly I’m speaking from experience (more laughter).

“And then I had that day that I woke up and I thought, ‘This book isn’t going to work.’ And people said to me, ‘You must have been so devastated.’ And yet it was physically walking out of the fog. My brain — that 40% of my brain that just couldn’t bear to look and see what I was really doing — came back to me and I was fully present in my life and in my body. And I was so glad that book was gone and that I could immediately with my full brain — it’s still pretty good, it turns out — write a book.”

That new book was “Whistler,” a No. 1 New York Times bestseller.

No matter where her hand mixer is these days, Ann Patchett still knows where to find the good stories.
