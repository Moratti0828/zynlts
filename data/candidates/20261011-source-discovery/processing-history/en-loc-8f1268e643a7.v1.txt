The 2026 National Book Festival arrived in style in late August, drawing tens of thousands of bibliophiles, many of whom turned up in festive shirts emblazoned with mottos such as “reading is my favorite sport,” “unreliable narrator,” “sorry, I’m booked” and “bookmarks are for quitters.”

More than 45,000 people attended this year’s event (Aug. 22) at the D.C. convention center, which showcased more than 80 authors and a pavillion full of programs featuring the Library’s collections and resources.

“The promise of a great library is that it opens doors,” said Acting Librarian of Congress Robert R. Newlen, in a short speech that opened the festival. “The Library of Congress has more doors to open than any other library in the world and we hope that you will walk through many of them. Today we have the extraordinary privilege of bringing together some of the most compelling writers and thinkers of our time with all of you, the people who love their work.”

At festival stages throughout the day, presenters spoke to the value of books and the knowledge held at the Library.

Author Tayari Jones said the event showed people continue to love reading. Fellow panelist Ann Patchett , who received the 2026 Library of Congress Prize for American Fiction at the festival, echoed her: “I’m so grateful to you for being here and for reading books.”

2025-2026 National Ambassador for Young People’s Literature Mac Barnett described exploring first editions and original printings of some of his favorite children’s books in the Library’s collections.

“It’s one of the places that kids and adults can meet as equals,” he said of children’s books.

Readers of all ages roamed the festival, including one young attendee who was so absorbed that he was walking around while keeping his nose in an open book.

On the expo floor, attendees perused an exhibit of posters from every year of the NBF — 25 from the past, plus a massive display of this year’s by Andrew Bannecker, where festivalgoers posed for photos.

Staff experts brought the Library to the convention center through a series of presentations at the Library of Congress Pavilion, as well as tables with interactive materials for guests.

Chronicling America helped kids put together zines and comics from the collection of digitized historical American newspapers. Visitors tested their knowledge with trivia from the U.S. Copyright Office, or matched books to the country of their authors with the Acquisitions and Bibliographic Access Directorate. Others practiced their librarian skills by creating authority records for their own names.

Kristen Reichenbach, a reference librarian in the Manuscript Division, highlighted the papers of Nellie Simmons Meier, a palmist who studied hands to read someone’s character. Meier collected handprints from notable contemporaries like Susan B. Anthony, Booker T. Washington and Anna May Wong, and attendees could compare their own palms with laminated facsimiles.

“It’s great for people to be able to touch history, as it were,” Reichenbach said.

The Collections Digitization Division presented at the festival for the first time this year. Among a group of collection items set to enter the public domain in 2027, visitors voted for what to digitize first by placing the item in the scanning bed and pressing a button to take a photo. (This author opted for “Dog-basket” by Diana Thorne.) All will ultimately be scanned and made available with millions of other collections online.

In the Workshop Space tucked away on the first floor of the convention center, an audience sat around tables to hear from the 2026 Academy of American Poets Laureate Fellows, offering cheers and reactions to the readings. Later in the day, the space hosted a hugely popular fiction writing workshop with novelist Angie Kim. She led participants in exercises on writing hooks, characters and voice, describing the story as the “tip of the iceberg” when it came to building the world of a novel.

The craft of writing was a theme woven across many panels by published authors.

“Your creative mind is like a friendly dog, and it brings you things,” said Elana K. Arnold, author of the young adult novel “Holloway.”

For humor writer Jenny Lawson, it’s a slow process — she said it typically takes her three to five years to write each book. Jones, the author of “Kin,” recalled returning to a spiral-bound notebook and pencil to write like she did as a kid.

“I write two sentences and then I watch 25 minutes of carpentry videos on YouTube,” said Kate McKinnon . (Relatable.)

Over the course of the 12-hour day, hundreds of volunteers in red shirts kept smiling and bringing the joy of reading to attendees.

“I’ve worked at the Library for over 10 years as an archivist and this is consistently one of the highlights of my job,” said Amanda Loeb, who served as main stage captain for this year’s festival.
