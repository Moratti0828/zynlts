Dolly Parton, the beloved country music legend and pop culture icon who died Tuesday, was also a tireless advocate for children’s literacy whose Imagination Library was given one of Library’s highest honors, part of her long association with the national library.

In the 2021 Library of Congress Literacy Awards , the Imagination Library, an initiative of Parton’s Dollywood Foundation, was awarded the David M. Rubenstein Prize of $150,000 for the program’s phenomenal national and international success.

“Dolly Parton’s work was precisely what we envisioned this prize rewarding,” Rubenstein said. “A well-run, highly dedicated literacy program that operated both with passion and a clear sense of mission. It was a privilege to know her and her work.”

The program started in Parton’s home county in rural east Tennessee in 1995 with 1,760 children under age 5 enrolled. Each child received one free book mailed to their home monthly for up to 60 months; therefore each child would have their own library when they started first grade.

By March of 2021, more than 1.8 million books were being mailed every month to children in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia and dozens of Indigenous communities across the world.

Three years earlier, Parton came to the Library to spend the day. She donated the 100 millionth copy of books shipped to children (it is now more than 330 million), sat for an interview with then-Librarian of Congress Carla Hayden and read to kids in the Great Hall. The photo of the reading, at the top of this story, is today one of the iconic images of the Library at one of its grandest and most fundamental missions — building and supporting a lifelong love of reading for all children.

“Due to our collections and their importance, we get a number of well-known people in the Library,” said Robert R. Newlen, the acting Librarian of Congress, “but when Dolly Parton came in the building that day it was like all the electricity came on at once. Her reading, and then singing, part of ‘Coat of Many Colors’ to the kids was truly special.”

The literacy work was special to Parton and her family, too, she told Hayden, particularly to her father, who was illiterate.

“My dad got to live long enough to see a lot of it come into being,” she said. “He took such pride in the fact that the little kids call me the ‘book lady.’ He was prouder of that than anything. So, it started from a true place in my heart , and it’s done so much good for so many people through the years.”

The Literacy Awards were just one of the many ways that Parton’s life and work crossed paths with the Library, some of which were highlighted in her 2020 documentary, “ The Library that Dolly Built. ”

In 2004, the Library put together an online presentation, “ Dolly Parton and the Roots of Country Music ,” that showed how her rise from tiny Sevierville, Tennessee, to worldwide stardom in the 1960s and 1970s coincided with the rise of the global popularity of country music. It includes many of her original submissions for song copyrights.

In 2014, the Literacy Awards recognized the Imagination Library with one of its Best Practices awards , recognizing the program’s efficiency and commitment to early childhood education.

And, of course, “Coat of Many Colors,” one of her signature songs, was added to the National Recording Registry in 2011. (She wrote “I Will Always Love You,” which was also inducted in 2019, as recorded by Whitney Houston.)

The 1971 guitar-and-bass ballad, sung in her tell-tale twangy earnestness, was about a painful incident when she was growing up in rural poverty in the Smoky Mountains foothills in the 1950s. The aching feeling built in her for years, she told the Library’s Cary O’Dell.

The song tells the story of how Parton’s mother took donated rags and sewed them into a patchwork coat for her. Parton loved her mother, adored the coat and proudly wore it to school — only to be ridiculed by classmates:

So with patches on my britches

And holes in both my shoes

In my coat of many colors

I hurried off to school

Just to find the others laughing

And making fun of me

My mama made for me.

“This experience had a big impact on me , so the painful part of the memory would come to the surface every now and again, then I would push it back down and then it would come around the bend once again,” she told O’Dell. “What is really remarkable about the whole journey was the fact that I carried that hurt for so long but, once I wrote the song, I felt released from the hurt. It allowed me to take something bad and turn it into a message of understanding and acceptance.”

And, as was her seemingly effortless gift, she was able to translate her specific experience in the early 1950s in a rural corner of America to a near universal understanding decades later of how to become and remain your own person, regardless of age, gender, race, sexual orientation or political affiliation.

It is difficult today to think of a more universally adored person in national life than Parton, and the reason seems clear: She wore her sincerity and decency on her sleeve, particularly her interest in children’s self-acceptance, and never backed away, modified or apologized for how straightforward she was about it.

All this could, in the 1960s and 1970s, what with her huge wigs and outré stage outfits, give the initial appearance of naivete or a lack of sophistication. It took the nation time to appreciate the depth and sincerity of who and what she was: Simply one of the great Americans of our age.

“Today,” she said in that 2016 interview with O’Dell, “lots of teachers tell me they use the song (“Coat”) to combat bullying in school because it captures the hurt. But at the same time, kids can see that you can rise above that hurt by pulling on your family, your friends, your faith and your own self-worth.”
